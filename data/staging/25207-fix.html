EES and ETIAS for UK nationals: key points

  • The EU Entry/Exit System (EES) registers non-EU nationals on short stays at the external borders of 29 European countries, Spain included, and it has been fully operational at all of them since 10 April 2026, as the European Commission announced.
  • A UK national on a short stay gives a facial image and fingerprints on the first crossing, the passport is not stamped, and the records of entries, exits and refusals are kept for three years from the date they were created.
  • The 90/180 rule is unchanged: a short stay is up to 90 days within any 180-day period, calculated as a single period for all the countries using the EES, and the EES identifies overstayers.
  • Holders of residence permits and long-stay visas are not registered in the EES, and UK nationals who are Withdrawal Agreement beneficiaries are exempt only if they hold a specific Withdrawal Agreement residence document.
  • As at 6 October 2026, the official ETIAS website states that ETIAS is not in operation, that no applications are collected, and that the EU will announce the start date several months before launch.
  • When ETIAS starts, the application fee is €20, which applicants under 18 or over 70 do not pay, and an authorisation is valid for three years or until the passport expires, whichever comes first.
  • Owning property in Spain does not by itself exempt a UK national from the EES or ETIAS, because the official exemption list covers residence permit and long-stay visa holders, not owners.

How the 2026 border changes affect UK nationals in Spain

UK nationals have been third-country nationals for Schengen purposes since the end of the Brexit transition period on 31 December 2020. Our guide on what is expected of British expats in Spain after Brexit covers that change.

The EES began a progressive start on 12 October 2025, according to the Commission’s news item of 30 March 2026. The Commission’s EES FAQ says that this phase lasted until 9 April 2026 and that the system has been fully operational at all external border crossing points since 10 April 2026.

ETIAS is a separate system. It is a pre-travel authorisation, and as at 6 October 2026 it has no confirmed start date.

What is the EU Entry/Exit System (EES)?

The EES is an automated IT system for registering non-EU nationals travelling for a short stay each time they cross the external borders of the countries using it. The Commission’s EES page lists 29 countries, and Spain is one of them.

The legal basis is Regulation (EU) 2017/2226. According to the Commission’s page on data held by the EES, the system collects, records and stores:

  • The data listed in the travel document, such as full name and date of birth.
  • The date and place of each entry and exit.
  • The facial image and fingerprints, called biometric data.
  • Whether the traveller was refused entry.

A traveller who does not need a short-stay visa has four fingerprints and a facial image stored. The Commission adds that the fingerprints of children below 12 years old are currently not scanned.

The retention periods in the same Commission page are:

  • Records of entries, exits and refusals of entry: 3 years from the date they were created.
  • Individual files containing personal data: 3 years and 1 day from the date of the last exit or of the refusal of entry.
  • Where no exit has been recorded: 5 years from the expiry date of the authorised stay.

A traveller who refuses to give biometric data is denied entry, according to the same page. A refusal of entry is recorded in the EES, so border officers in the countries using the system can see it.

Why the EES and ETIAS matter for UK nationals

Free movement for UK citizens ended on 31 December 2020. The EES applies to every non-EU national who does not need a visa for a short stay, and that includes UK visitors on tourism, business meetings, family visits and second-home stays.

The practical implications are these:

  • Passport stamps are replaced by the electronic record. The Commission states that a passport is not stamped when the traveller is registered, although Cyprus and Ireland still stamp passports manually.
  • The system works out the remaining authorised stay. The Commission offers an online tool, Check how long you can stay, and border officers can also tell the traveller the remaining duration.
  • A traveller who stays longer than permitted is identified as an overstayer, and the data is added to a list that border and immigration authorities can access.
  • The consequences depend on national law. The Commission mentions removal, administrative fines, detention and a bar on re-entering the EU, and the UK government warns of a ban of up to 3 years from Schengen countries for overstaying.

How the EES works at Spanish borders

On the first crossing since the EES started, the traveller provides personal data, and border officers take a photo of the face and, where required, scan the fingerprints. The Commission explains in how the EES works that part of the data can be registered in advance on self-service equipment or in a mobile app, where the country offers one.

On later crossings, officers verify the fingerprints and photo already stored, which takes less time. Travellers with a biometric passport can use self-service systems where they are available, and in rare cases the data has to be collected again.

The Commission reported on 10 April 2026 that more than 52 million entries and exits had been registered and there had been over 27 000 refusals of entry since the roll-out began.

Gibraltar is a separate case. The UK government states that, from 15 July 2026, the UK-EU Agreement in respect of Gibraltar provisionally applies, that entry conditions for short-stay visitors align with Schengen requirements, and that time spent in Gibraltar counts towards the 90/180 limit. The details for residents and cross-border workers depend on that agreement, so please contact us to confirm your case.

The 90/180 rule and EES enforcement

A short stay is up to 90 days within any 180-day period, and the Commission explains that this period is calculated as a single period for all the European countries using the EES. For UK nationals the rule has applied since 1 January 2021.

Before the EES, border officers relied on manual passport stamps to check the rule. The EES now registers each entry and exit electronically and calculates the remaining stay.

This matters most for UK second-home owners without a residence permit, who can spend no more than 90 days in any 180-day period in Spain and the rest of the countries using the EES together. The system records any overstay, and the consequences depend on national law, as described above.

ETIAS: start date, fee and who needs it

ETIAS, the European Travel Information and Authorisation System, is a pre-travel authorisation for visa-exempt travellers, and it is separate from the EES. While the EES records who crosses the border and when, ETIAS is checked before boarding.

As at 6 October 2026, the official ETIAS FAQ states that ETIAS is not in operation, that no applications are being collected and that the EU will announce the specific start date several months before launch. Earlier Commission news items, dated 21 July 2025 and 16 April 2026, said that visa-exempt travellers, UK nationals included, would need ETIAS from the last quarter of 2026. We have not found a confirmed start date on the official site, and we will update this post when the EU announces one.

The fee is €20. The Commission’s news item of 21 July 2025 states that the fee increased from the previous €7, and sources that still quote €7 are out of date. Applicants under 18 or over 70 do not pay the fee, according to the official FAQ, and the fee is not refunded if the application is unsuccessful.

Once ETIAS starts, applications can be made on the official ETIAS website or in the official mobile app. The Commission warns that many unofficial websites exist and that some charge higher fees, and no application can be made today on any website, because ETIAS is not in operation.

Key things to know about ETIAS:

  • It applies to UK nationals and the nationals of other visa-exempt countries and territories, and it covers 30 European countries.
  • Most applications are processed within minutes, and at the latest within 96 hours, unless the applicant has to provide more information or attend an interview, which can add up to 30 days.
  • An authorisation is valid for three years or until the passport expires, whichever comes first, and each traveller, including children, applies individually.
  • Travellers who hold a residence card or a residence permit issued by a country requiring ETIAS, including a Withdrawal Agreement residence document with the exception of Cyprus, do not need ETIAS, and neither do holders of a valid visa. A Withdrawal Agreement document issued by Ireland does not exempt its holder.
  • A valid authorisation does not guarantee entry, because the traveller still passes border checks and the 90/180 limit still applies.

UK property owners and second-home holders

The combination of the EES and the 90/180 rule is most relevant for UK nationals who own property in Spain but do not hold a Spanish residence permit. Owners of a Costa Blanca apartment, a Valencia city flat or an Andalusian villa count their days like any other visitor.

The options for this group are limited but clear:

  • Stay within 90/180. Track your days carefully and use the Commission’s Check how long you can stay tool before each trip.
  • Apply for a long-stay visa. The Non-Lucrative Visa is the classic route for retirees and remote workers without Spanish employment, and the Digital Nomad Visa works for those who can demonstrate remote employment.
  • Check your tax residency. A residence permit does not by itself decide tax residency, which follows the tests in article 9 of the Spanish Personal Income Tax Act. This post covers Spanish and EU rules, so coordinate your UK tax filing with your UK adviser, and see our UK-Spain double taxation guide.
  • Sell or rent out the property. For those who used the property as a holiday home without any intention of becoming resident, selling may be the right answer.

Each option has tax, immigration and asset-planning implications that need to be assessed together rather than in isolation.

FAQs: what UK travellers ask in 2026

Do I need to register for the EES before I travel?

You are registered at the border on your first entry. Where the border offers self-service equipment or a mobile app, part of the data can be registered in advance, and the UK government states that there is no cost for EES registration.

ETIAS is a different system. As at 6 October 2026 it is not in operation, so there is nothing to apply for yet.

How long is my EES record kept?

Records of entries, exits and refusals of entry are kept for 3 years from the date they were created, and the individual file for 3 years and 1 day from your last exit or refusal. If no exit has been recorded, the period is 5 years from the expiry of your authorised stay, and the data is then erased automatically.

Will my passport still be stamped?

No, the Commission states that a registered traveller’s passport is not stamped. Cyprus and Ireland still stamp passports manually.

Can my UK family members and I register together at the EES kiosk?

Each traveller is registered individually. The Commission states that the fingerprints of children below 12 are currently not scanned.

What happens if I overstay the 90/180 limit?

The EES identifies you as an overstayer and adds your data to a list that border and immigration authorities can access. The consequences depend on national law and can include removal, fines, detention or a bar on re-entry, and the UK government warns of a ban of up to 3 years from Schengen countries.

Do I need to pay €20 for ETIAS every time I travel?

No, an ETIAS authorisation is valid for three years or until the passport expires, whichever comes first, and covers multiple trips. ETIAS is not in operation as at 6 October 2026, and applicants under 18 or over 70 do not pay the fee.

Am I exempt from the EES if I have property in Spain?

Property ownership alone does not exempt you from the EES. The exemptions cover holders of residence permits and long-stay visas, among others, and not owners who visit on short stays.

Can I apply for Spanish residency to avoid EES registration?

UK nationals who obtain a long-stay visa and then a residence document are not registered in the EES. The main routes are the Non-Lucrative Visa for retirees and passive-income holders, the Digital Nomad Visa for remote workers, and a work visa for those moving for Spanish employment.

What documents should I carry at the Spanish border in 2026?

The UK government lists a passport with an expiry date at least 3 months after the day you plan to leave the Schengen area and a date of issue less than 10 years before you arrive. You may also need to show a return or onward ticket, proof of travel insurance, evidence that you have enough money for your stay, and a hotel booking or proof of address.

UK residents in Spain should carry their TIE card alongside their passport, as the same UK government page advises.
